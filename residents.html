Resident information.

Who these communities are for, what daily life looks like, and how joining will work. Applications are not open yet. When they open, the list hears first.

Who this is for.

Women in or approaching retirement who want their independence and have no interest in spending the next twenty years alone with it. Women who can manage their own day and would rather not manage a roof, a lawn and a furnace as well.

Most residents will come from a house that has become too much, a marriage that ended or a partner who died, or simply a decision to choose their surroundings on purpose for the first time in decades.

  • Not assisted living. No one is on staff to help with dressing, bathing or medication.
  • No nursing care on site. If your needs change, you arrange care as you would in any home you own.
  • No schedule. Dinners and gatherings are open invitations. Skipping them costs you nothing.
  • Not a rental. You own your home and hold a real voice in how the community is run.

What daily life looks like.

Your home is yours, and most of the day belongs to you alone. The common house is where the day widens out: a kitchen worth cooking in, a long table, a room with a fire, guest rooms for your family, a studio, and a terrace where the evenings tend to end.

Cooking rotates. Several residents sign up for a night and the rest come and eat. On nights nobody has signed up, the kitchen is still yours.

  • A private home you own

    One level, step free, with your own kitchen, laundry and a covered patio facing the green.

  • The common kitchen and long table

    Shared meals several nights a week, cooked on a rotation, in a kitchen built for several people at once.

  • Guest rooms for your family

    So children and grandchildren can stay the weekend without anyone sleeping on a sofa.

  • A studio and a work room

    For projects, for making things, and for the work that did not end when the job did.

  • Grounds and gardens

    A kitchen garden, shaded walks, and a raised bed of your own if you want one.

  • Nothing to maintain

    Roof, exterior, grounds and shared systems are handled and paid for from the monthly fee.

What it costs.

You should understand this in one reading. If any part is unclear, the fault is ours.

$50,000 to $100,000 A one time buy in, paid on joining. The figure depends on the community, the home and its siting.
Monthly fee Covers upkeep, insurance and reserves for the common house and grounds, all exterior maintenance, and shared utilities. Figure to be confirmed.
Your own utilities Power, water and internet for your own home are billed to you, as anywhere else.
When you go Your interest may be sold to an incoming resident. You will see exactly how that works, in writing, well before you commit.

These are working figures while the communities are being formed. Nothing on this page is an offer or a contract. Before anyone puts money in, you will receive the full documents to read at your own pace and to take to your own attorney and your own family.

How joining will work.

Applications are not open yet. This is the process as we intend it.

  • 1. Join the list

    No cost, no obligation. You hear first when applications open in a given community.

  • 2. A conversation

    Several, in fact, without pressure. We talk about what you want and whether this is really it.

  • 3. A visit

    Come and stay a night or two once the common house is standing, and meet the women already committed.

  • 4. The documents

    The full terms, in writing, to take to your own attorney and your family with as much time as you need.

  • 5. Choosing your home

    Founding residents choose first, and help shape decisions the rest will live with for years.

Questions worth asking early.

Do I own my home, or am I renting?

You own it. Your buy in gives you the home and a share of the land, the common house and the grounds, along with a vote in decisions that affect everyone.

What happens if my health changes?

You handle it as you would in any home you own, with your doctors, your family, and in home help if you need it. Your neighbors will drive you to an appointment and bring you supper. They are not nurses, and we will never pretend otherwise.

If you reach a point of needing daily medical care, this will not be the right place to remain. We would far rather you know that now than discover it later.

Is there a kitchen in my home?

Yes. Every home has its own kitchen, compact and properly equipped, with an island and dining area in the larger plan. You are never obliged to eat with anyone.

The common house has a second kitchen, much larger, built for several cooks at once. That is where the shared dinners happen. Having both is deliberate: the choice is yours every night rather than made for you by the floor plan.

Do I have to eat dinner with everyone?

No. Come to all of them, one a month, or none. Nobody keeps count and nobody asks. Be honest with yourself about it though, since meals sit closer to the center of life here than in an ordinary neighborhood.

May I bring my dog?

Yes. Dogs and cats are welcome in your home and on the walks, with a few sensible rules about the garden beds and leads on the green.

Where do my children and grandchildren sleep?

In the guest rooms in the common house, reserved ahead on the shared calendar. Your family can stay a week without anyone being underfoot.

Is there staff?

Maintenance, landscaping and housekeeping of the common house are contracted and paid from the monthly fee. Nobody lives on site to look after you, and nobody is watching you either.

Is this only for women?

Residency is intended for women in or approaching retirement. Husbands, partners, children and grandchildren are welcome as guests at any time.
